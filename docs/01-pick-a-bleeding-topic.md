# Chapter 1: Pick a Topic That Hurts People Today

## The idea

The best manuals are written for a pain, not about a subject. "AI" is a subject.
"How to have your dinner-planning conversation with a local AI chat so you stop
dreading the question" is a pain. Pain has a person, a moment, and a cost. Write
there.

## What "hurts today" means

A topic hurts people today when someone, right now, pays one of these costs:

- **Time tax.** They spend hours doing something that should take minutes.
- **Confusion tax.** They are afraid to touch the tool at all, so they avoid the
  whole area of life around it.
- **Money tax.** They pay someone else, or they pay for the wrong thing.
- **Dignity tax.** They feel stupid, and that feeling is the real blocker.

The dignity tax is the one beginner-facing writers forget. A lot of "simple" tasks
are simple only after someone explains the vocabulary, the order of operations, and
the parts everyone pretends are obvious. If your reader silently feels dumb, they
quit not because the manual failed but because the manual never addressed the feeling.

## How to find your topic

**1. Mine your own last two weeks.** What did you help someone with that took
longer than it should have? What did you do three times before it clicked? What
did you explain twice on the phone?

**2. Listen for phrases that signal pain.**

- "Where do I even start with…"
- "Is it supposed to ask me that?"
- "I gave up and paid for…"
- "I don't want to break anything."

Each phrase is a chapter title wearing a disguise.

**3. Check the pain is:

- **Real:** someone besides you has it (a friend, a forum thread, a coworker, your
  own logged wasted hours).
- **Recurring:** it happens weekly or monthly, not once.
- **Reachable:** you can actually describe the fix in plain English, today.
- **Safe:** your manual can't hurt anyone if they follow it wrong. Education,
  never medical, legal, or financial advice; say so when your topic brushes those.

## Rank candidates honestly

Score each candidate 1 (low) to 5 (high) on four axes and pick the highest total:

- **Hurt:** how much does it cost the person per week in time, money, or dread?
- **Audience:** how many people you can actually reach have this pain?
- **Clarity:** can you explain the fix without hand-waving?
- **Freshness:** can a manual written this month still be true next quarter?

Tie-break toward the topic you could demo to a friend tonight. Enthusiasm is
strategy: the book that excites you gets finished, and finished books help people,
while great unfinished ones help no one.

## The one-sentence pain test

Before you commit, write the topic as a sentence of this shape:

> "This book is for **[person]** who is stuck on **[specific moment]** and wants
> **[specific outcome]** without **[the fear or tax they're avoiding]**."

If you cannot fill that in without weaselly words like "understanding" or
"learning about," the topic is still a subject, not a pain. Keep digging. When the
sentence reads something like *"This book is for delivery drivers who are stuck
setting up free captioning apps and want readable transcripts in ten minutes
without buying anything,"* you have found a chapter-able pain.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing impressiveness.** Advanced topics feel more respectable. They help
  fewer people per page.
- **Picking a moving target.** If your topic's facts change weekly, you've
  productized a news cycle. Pick the stable pain underneath (setup, safety,
  backup) rather than the unstable surface (this week's headline).
- **Writing for the version of you who already knows.** Your reader is the
  two-weeks-younger you, before the clicks made sense.
