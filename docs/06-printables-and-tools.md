# Chapter 6: Turn Chapters into Printables and Tool Ideas

## The idea

A book teaches at one speed and at one desk. A printable teaches anywhere:
 taped to a locker, clipped to a fridge, in a drawer at work. A small tool
remembers what the book taught and carries it when the reader can't. One
chapter can honestly become four artifacts if you write with this in mind.

## The artifact ladder

For every chapter, walk down this ladder and stop where it stops being honest
(for the chapter, not for the book overall):

1. **Facts page** — the chapter's core knowledge in one page, for reference
   after the tutorial is forgotten.
2. **Checklist** — the steps, nothing but ordered steps, printable, checkboxes
   for the reader's pen.
3. **Cheat sheet** — the vocabulary, the screen names, the buttons, the error
   messages and what they mean, on one card.
4. **Session card** — "what to do when you do this thing again": the 10-minute
   repeatable routine, for when they return in six weeks and forgot everything.
5. **Template** — the fill-in-the-blank version of whatever the reader
   produces (a prompt template, a budget table, a maintenance log).
6. **Tool** — a tiny program or spreadsheet that does the tedious part
   automatically. Only when the tedium is real and the rules are stable.

Ladder climbing rule: each rung must stand alone. A checklist that requires
reading the chapter isn't a checklist; it's a table of contents with delusions.

## What makes a printable work off the desk

- **Fits on one page** (US Letter and A4 — test both).
- **Prints in black and white** and still works. If color matters, say so in
  words next to where color would be, not as a color-only diagram.
- **Big enough to read.** 10.5pt body minimum for an older reader.
- **Named for the job:** "Weekly Dinner Planner," not "Appendix C-7."
- **Works after the book is gone.** No sentences that depend on chapter context.

## Turning a chapter into a tool idea

Small tools are how a manual keeps teaching after the reader stops reading. To
find the tool inside a chapter, ask three questions:

1. **What did the reader manually compute, count, sort, or copy-paste?** That's
   the tool's job description. (Planning a week's dinners → a one-screen meal
   planner sheet at first, a tiny script that formats a shopping list later.)
2. **What does the roadmap say "remember to check" about?** A tool that checks
   beats remembering. (Version numbers, backup dates, file sizes.)
3. **Where did the reader have to convert between formats or units?** That's a
   small converter, the simplest possible tool.

Size discipline for tools: a tool earns its place when it saves more minutes
per month than its own maintenance costs per year. For a beginner manual's
first edition, that means: single file, no accounts, works offline, no data
collection. If the tool needs a server, it's edition two (or three).

## A worked example

Take one grocery chapter and climb the ladder:

- **Facts page:** how the app handles lists, what the icon names mean.
- **Checklist:** 15 steps, from "open the app" to "shared list is on the fridge
  note."
- **Cheat sheet:** icon dictionary plus the three error messages the reader
  will actually see.
- **Session card:** "Your weekly reset, in 6 minutes" — the routine for doing
  it again next Friday.
- **Template:** a printable shopping list with the columns the chapter's
  workflow needs.
- **Tool idea (park in ideas.md):** a tiny script that turns the finished
  shopping list into an aisle-sorted version based on store layout.

Notice: the chapter didn't change. The artifacts grew around it.

## Practical workflow

While drafting each chapter keep a running section at the bottom (delete before
shipping): "artifact candidates." Every time you write a sentence worth
remembering separately — a warning, a check, a definition — drop it in the
candidates list. At the end of drafting, sort the list into the ladder, pick
two or three artifacts for the first edition, and park the rest. Chapters stay
chapters; artifacts multiply their reach.
