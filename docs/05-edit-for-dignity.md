# Chapter 5: Edit for Dignity and Warmth, Never Condescension

## The idea

Warmth is respect made visible. Condescension is pity wearing a smile. The line
between them is thin, and most manuals cross it by accident, usually in the second
draft, when the writer finally feels confident and starts talking down.

## The dignity principle

Assume the reader is intelligent, busy, and self-respecting, and that confusion
is situational, not personal. Write every page as if the person reading is
competent at their own life — they run households, jobs, and emergencies — and is
merely new to *your* small corner of it. That assumption should be audible in
every sentence.

The test: **would this sentence survive being read aloud to a competent friend
who's new to the tool?** If you'd blush saying it, cut it.

## The three condescension patterns to hunt

**1. Emphasis words that imply the reader struggled needlessly.**

- "It's *really* easy once you see it." → Cut "really easy." Show, don't grade.
- "You *just* click here." → The word "just" grades the reader.
- "Don't panic." → Commands an emotion; instead say what's true: "Nothing here
  is permanent. Everything can be undone."

**2. Fake enthusiasm ("Great job! You opened a menu!").**

Praise costs trust. If the reader has done something genuinely tricky — wrote
their first script, migrated their photos — one specific acknowledgment is warm.
"Opening a menu" is not an achievement; reformatting a decade of family video
is. Calibrate praise to actual difficulty, and make it specific:
"if this is your first time editing a config file, that genuinely was the hard part.
The rest is smoother."

**3. Baby steps on adult topics.**

Adults managing medications, money, or legal paperwork do not need sing-song
structure or emoji confetti; they need clarity and respect. Keep the plain
sentence shapes from Chapter 3 and raise the register slightly: fewer exclamations,
more steadiness. Warmth there sounds like a calm companion, not a cartoon.

## Where warmth actually comes from

Warmth is a set of habits, not adjectives:

- **Anticipate embarrassment and normalize it in advance.** "Everyone's first
  attempt at this looks wrong. Here's what 'wrong but on track' looks like."
- **Admit your own history.** "The first time I did this, I deleted the wrong
  file and lost an evening. Here's the check that prevents that."
- **Name the emotional beat, then control it.** "If your stomach tightens when
  you see that the export is empty, that's your cue to check the folder path
  before anything else." The reader feels seen, not judged.
- **Waste the reader's time never.** Respecting their time is the warmest thing
  a manual can do. Every unnecessary paragraph is a small act of disrespect.
- **Keep humor humane.** Joke about the situation or about yourself; never
  about the reader, the software's victims, or people's learning.

## The revision pass that finds all of this

Do a dedicated read-through with one job: hunt tone.

1. Read only the first sentences of paragraphs in order. Do they assume a
   competent, tired person?
2. Search for the grading words: *just, simply, easy, obvious, basic, actually,
   even I could*. Cut or rewrite each.
3. Search for exclamation marks. Two per chapter, maximum, and they must earn it.
4. Search for every place you say "you" followed by a mistake ("you'll probably
   mess up"). Replace with the situation: "this step fails when the file is
   still locked."
5. Read one section aloud. Anywhere you hear yourself lecturing, soften the
   frame but keep the facts.

## Word-level swaps

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Don't worry" | "Nothing here is permanent." |
| "Even a beginner can do this" | "This takes about five minutes and no special knowledge." |
| "Great job!" | (nothing) or, for genuinely hard steps, name what was hard |
| "As you can see…" | "Your screen now shows ___." (say what you can see) |
| "You should average about X" (hovering) | "Most people need X on the first try." |
| "The gentle art of…" | Name the thing: "How to ___" |

## The stance summary

Write every page to the reader a nurse would talk to at the end of a double
shift: sharp, tired, allergic to being handled, fully capable once the fog is
lifted. Clarity is the kindness. Confidence without grading is the warmth.
Condescension is the only unforgivable tone in beginner writing, because it
kicks the person while the tool already did.
