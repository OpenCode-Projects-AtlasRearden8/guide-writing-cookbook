# Glossary

Every term this cookbook uses that a normal person might reasonably not know yet,
defined plainly, in alphabetical order.

**Anchor (in a lesson)** — The first one-to-three sentences of a lesson that name
where the reader is and what they'll have when it ends.

**Artifact** — Anything a chapter becomes besides the chapter itself: a printable
checklist, a cheat sheet, a session card, a template, a small tool. Chapter 6
teaches the ladder.

**Blocker** — The thing stopping the reader right now. Good manuals name it in the
reader's own words, then remove it.

**Cheat sheet** — A one-page card holding the vocabulary, screen names, and error
messages of one task. See the artifact ladder.

**Cheat sheet vs. session card** — A cheat sheet answers "what does this screen
say?"; a session card answers "how do I do this again, six weeks from now?"

**Check (a check step)** — A step or sentence where the reader confirms something
worked, by looking at something specific. "Your file list now shows the new
recording" is a check; "it worked" is not.

**Claim** — Any sentence in your book that asserts the world is a certain way.
Facts, procedures, implications, comparisons, forecasts. Chapter 7 sorts them.

**Condescension** — Tone that assumes the reader is less capable than they are:
grading words ("just", "easy"), fake praise, baby steps on adult topics. The one
unforgivable tone in beginner writing.

**First edition** — The smallest true version of a manual you can finish this
week: 3–6 chapters, 15–40 pages, one project taught to done.

**Hub site** — A simple web home for your work, built from static files
(no server logic), like the one at `dylanasdale.github.io`.

**Jargon** — A word your reader will meet in the software's screens before they
meet it in your book. Must be defined at first use, in that sentence.

**Local-first** — Built so everything works from files you own: no accounts, no
servers required, no data sent anywhere, works offline after download.

**Meta-statement** — A claim about your own book ("this chapter takes 20
minutes"). These date fastest; date them like milk.

**Plain English** — Writing that a tired adult can act on without asking anyone:
short sentences, shared vocabulary, specific numbers, no grading words.

**Printable** — A page designed to be printed and used away from the desk, test-
ed in black and white on both US Letter and A4.

**Procedure** — A claim of the form "do X, then Y." The only reliable verification
is doing it fresh, on a clean state, following only your own steps.

**Recovery path** — The instruction that tells the reader how to undo or get out,
placed before or beside the risky step. Fear of irreversibility is the biggest
reason readers quit.

**Rhythm** — The steady repeatable beat of a lesson (anchor, setup, steps, check,
breathe, next). Predictable rhythm is what carries tired readers past their fear.

**Scope** — The exact boundary of what your first edition promises to teach. Small
enough to finish; honest enough to verify.

**Session card** — A printable for repeating a task: the 6–10 minute routine for
doing it again after you forgot the details.

**Static site** — A website made of plain HTML/CSS files with nothing running on
a server. Fast, free to host, and it works if you copy it anywhere.

**Step group** — Steps 1 through 5, as the reader experiences them: one screen, one
column, one breath. The unit of difficulty.

**Verification loop** — Write, reset your state, follow only your own steps fresh,
log what actually happened, fix, rerun entirely.

**Volatile claim** — A claim that decays: version numbers, prices, free-tier
limits, "currently X." Every one gets a date checked and a next check.
