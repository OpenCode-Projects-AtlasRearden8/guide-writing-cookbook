# Chapter 4: Step-by-Step Lesson Rhythms

## The idea

Readers don't experience your book as chapters. They experience it as rhythm —
the predictable beat of explain, do, check, breathe. Rhythm is what lets a tired
person keep going, because they always know what kind of page comes next.

## The core rhythm

Every lesson beats in the same pattern:

1. **Anchor (1–3 sentences).** Name where the reader is and what they'll leave
   with. "In the next ten minutes you'll have a working transcript of your first
   audio file."
2. **Setup (short).** The three things the reader needs on screen: the app, the
   file, the state. No surprises hidden later.
3. **Steps (numbered, same shape as Chapter 3's template).** Action, expectation,
   why-if-useful, check.
4. **Check (a full stop, literally).** A sentence where the reader confirms it
   worked. "Your file list now shows the new recording, with a file size under
   2 MB."
5. **Breathe (a full paragraph or a small aside).** Tell them something true
   about how it felt: "The first time takes 4 minutes because the model loads
   once; later transcriptions are faster."
6. **What's next (one sentence).** Forward motion without cliffhangers.

Repeat the beat consistently. Varying the pattern forces the reader to re-learn
your book every chapter; keeping it makes reading frictionless.

## Beat lengths matter as much as beat order

- **Setup never longer than 5 steps.** If it is, the setup is its own lesson.
- **Steps in groups of 5 or fewer,** separated by a heading or a check. A wall
  of 23 steps is where readers quit.
- **A check at least every 5 steps.** The reader should never travel more than
  a page without a confirmation point.
- **One "why" paragraph per lesson.** People can follow steps blind, but one
  honest reason per lesson builds the trust that carries them through step 4 of
  hour 2.

## Handling the tired reader

Design lessons for the person whose brain has one opportunity left today:

- **Front-load the win.** Get them to something that visibly works inside the
  first 10 minutes of the whole book.
- **Natural stopping points every half hour.** Rest points are features. Say so:
  "Good stopping place. Chapter 5 is another session."
- **Recovery always within reach.** If they botch a step, the next paragraph or
  a nearby box tells them how to undo it. Fear of irreversibility is the big
  goodbye; recovery is the welcome-back sign.
- **No unread prerequisites.** If a lesson truly requires the previous one, say
  it before the lesson, not after three steps.

## Lesson genres and their rhythms

Different pages need different beats. Here are the four you'll use most, and how
they shift the template:

| Lesson type | Begin with | Emphasis | End with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Setup** | what you'll have at the end | exact states on screen | a check the reader can't fake |
| **Task** | the payoff sentence | numbered steps + checks | the finished artifact |
| **Concept** | the lie it fixes | feeling + example | one-line restatement |
| **Troubleshooter** | the symptom the reader has | if/then ladders | escalation path when nothing works |

Troubleshooting pages deserve special care because readers arrive frightened.
Match the symptom in their words, not yours: "Nothing happens when you click
Export" is a symptom. "Export handler errors" is not.

## Rhythm test

Read three consecutive lessons and ask:

- Did each one start by telling me where I am?
- Did one — never more than two — go longer without a check?
- Did at least one beat land as genuine (a joke, a kindness, an honest "this
  part is annoying")?
- Could I stop after any lesson and know things are in a state I can return to?

Three yeses out of four means your rhythm is working. Two means tired readers
will stall; find the lesson that breaks the beat and rebuild it.
