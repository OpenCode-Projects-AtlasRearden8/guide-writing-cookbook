# Chapter 3: Plain-English Drafting Rules

## The idea

Plain English is not "dumbed down." It is the fast lane. Every extra word makes
a tired reader more likely to bounce, and every show-off word makes them feel
excluded. Your job: the shortest sentence that is still true, specific, and kind.

## The fifteen rules

1. **Second person, present tense.** "Open the menu," not "the user should open
   the menu," not "you will then open the menu."
2. **One instruction per sentence.** Two tasks in one sentence is two chances to
   get lost.
3. **Sentences under 25 words as the default.** Long ones allowed, rarely, and
   on purpose.
4. **Words a tired 12-year-old knows.** Not for the sake of simplicity, but
   because that vocabulary is shared by every adult.
5. **Names for things, before verbs about them.** Introduce the "sidebar" as a
   thing before you ask the reader to "use the sidebar."
6. **Explain jargon on first use, in the sentence where it appears.** If you
   need a footnote to be understood, the sentence is broken.
7. **Active voice.** "The app saves your draft" beats "your draft is saved."
8. **Numbers over adjectives.** "About 40 seconds" beats "quickly."
9. **Screen text in quotes or code style, exactly as it appears.** `Settings`,
   "Export as PDF". Match capitalization even when the app is wrong about it.
10. **One level of nested clauses, maximum.** If a sentence has a clause inside
    a clause, it's two sentences.
11. **Say what happens, not just what to do.** "Click Save. The button turns
    gray for a second, which means it worked."
12. **Warn before, not after.** Put the danger note before the risky step, not
    as an apology afterward.
13. **Contractions welcome.** "You'll" reads friendlier than "you will." Write
    like a person.
14. **Cut these on sight:** *simply*, *just*, *easy*, *obviously*, *basically*,
    *merely*. They grade the wrong reader and add nothing to the right one.
15. **Read every paragraph out loud once.** Your ear catches what your eye forgives.

## The banned-words story

Words like *just* and *simply* seem harmless, but they carry a hidden judgment:
"this should have been easy for you." The reader who struggled for an hour hears
"you failed at something trivial." Delete the word, keep the sentence, lose the
insult. Same with "don't worry" — you cannot command worry away; you can only
tell the reader what's true at the moment that would have made them worry.

## Structure beats brilliance

Plain English lives in structure more than vocabulary:

- **Headlines that say what the reader gains,** not what the chapter is about.
  "Get your first transcript in ten minutes," not "Transcription overview."
- **Numbered steps for anything done in order.** Bullets are for orderless sets;
  if the reader has to guess the order, it's a numbered list, not a bullet list.
- **One paragraph, one idea.** First sentence carries it; the rest supports it.
- **White space is reader kindness.** Dense pages are unwelcoming, not thorough.

## The template for a step

A reliable shape for instructional steps:

1. **Action:** "Click the Settings gear."
2. **Expectation:** "A panel opens on the right."
3. **Why (when useful):** "This is where export options live."
4. **Check:** "You should see the version number at the bottom."

Not every step needs all four. Every confusing place needs at least the
expectation, because the reader's question is usually "did that work?"

## A before-and-after

**Before:** "Prior to initiating the export process, it is important that users
have first verified that their account has been properly configured with the
appropriate output settings in the preferences section of the application."

**After:** "Before you export, open Settings and check that Output Format is set
to `MP3`. If it isn't, set it now — the export will forget this choice otherwise."

Same information. Half the words. The reader can act immediately and knows why.

## Rule to break, and when

Keep a metaphor if it outperforms an explanation three times in a row. "An API
key is like a house key you hand to a housekeeper" may be the shortest true
explanation available. That's plain English too. The test is never "small words";
it is "can the reader do the next step without asking anyone."
