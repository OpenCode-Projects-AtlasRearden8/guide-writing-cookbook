# Chapter 2: Scope a First Edition You Can Finish

## The idea

An unfinished manual helps no one. A small, true, finished one helps everyone it
reaches. Scope is not lowering your standards; it is choosing which standard you
will meet: *done*.

## The first-edition test

Your first edition must pass all five:

1. **You can draft it in a week of evenings.** Count hours, not optimism. If your
   evenings give you 8 hours and the draft needs 30, the scope is wrong.
2. **Every page earns its place.** If a reader could finish the project without
   page 7, page 7 is edition two.
3. **You can verify everything in it yourself**, on hardware and accounts you
   control, before shipping.
4. **It stands alone.** No "see my future chapter 12 for the part that matters."
5. **It has a done-state.** You can say exactly what "finished" looks like:
   a page count, a printable, a working build.

## Cut with the one-project rule

A first edition teaches **one project, to done, with one mentioned alternative.**
Not one area. One project. Compare:

- Too wide: "Using AI for meal planning."
- One project: "Plan a week's dinners with a free AI chat in one 15-minute
  conversation, including the shopping list."
- Alternative mentioned in one paragraph: "and what to do if you hit a paywall."

## The chapter diet

A healthy first edition is 3 to 6 chapters, 15 to 40 pages total:

- **Chapter 0 or intro:** who this is for and what they'll have at the end (1 page).
- **The setup:** minimum steps to get the reader's hands on the tools you use.
- **The spine:** the actual task, broken into the sturdiest 2 to 4 chapters.
- **The recovery page:** what to do when it goes wrong, because it will.

Everything else — history, theory, comparisons to other tools, the cool tangent —
is sketch-level notes for edition two. Do not delete those notes; park them in a
file named `ideas.md`.

## Pitfalls that blow up scope

- **The completeness trap.** "I should cover every platform." No. Start with the
  one thing you verified, and say plainly it's about the tool you used.
- **The research spiral.** You do not need to read four academic papers to write
  a setup guide. You need to have done the setup.
- **The tool zoo.** Covering three tools triples your verify load. First edition:
  one primary path.
- **The hidden tutorial.** If your outline secretly assumes the reader already
  did something ("open your config" — which config? how?), shrink the scope until
  the hidden step is either explained or out the door.

## A scoping worksheet

Fill this in before you write. It takes ten minutes and saves weeks.

- **The project (one sentence):** the reader does X and gets Y.
- **Done looks like:** N chapters, M pages, Q printables, one build that passes.
- **Hours I actually have this week:** ___
- **The setup chapter's first sentence:** "By page 4 you will have ___ running."
- **The recovery page goes wrong-ways list:** the 3 most likely failure modes,
  from your own experience.
- **Edition-two parking lot:** the 3 things you most want to add, officially
  deferred, with no guilt.

## When the scope is right, you'll feel slightly disappointed

This is the honest signal. The scope that fits your week always looks a little
too small from the outside. Trust the math, not the ambition. A 20-page manual
that exists beats the 120-page one that would have been perfect.
